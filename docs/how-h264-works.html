How H.264 works

A video encoder is essentially a tool that tries to spend a limited number of bits where they matter most.

1What H.264 adds

Part one covers the fundamentals every block-based codec shares. The bit budget, the kinds of redundancy worth finding, the predict-transform-quantize-entropy loop and the reconstruction that feeds back into it, rate control, and how quality gets measured. This page is about what H.264 specifically does with it, and it assumes you have read the other.

The short version of the handoff. H.264 processes each frame in 16x16 macroblocks, and it brings a named tool to each kind of redundancy part one describes:

  • Spatial → intra prediction, nine directional modes at 4x4 and 8x8 plus four at 16x16, over an integer transform that is exact (intra, transform).
  • Temporal → motion compensation at quarter-pixel accuracy, up to sixteen reference frames, variable partitions and B-frame pyramids (frames, motion).
  • Statistical → CABAC, an adaptive arithmetic coder that re-estimates its probabilities as it goes (entropy coding).
  • Perceptual → not in the standard at all. It lives in the encoder's decisions, which is why two conformant H.264 encoders at the same bitrate can look so different, and it is most of what this project is about (the size of the choice).

One structural addition is worth flagging before the tour. Part one's loop ends by reconstructing the frame the next one predicts from. H.264 puts a deblocking filter inside that loop (deblocking), so encoder and decoder must filter identically or they drift apart. It is normative, not a post-process, and that decision shapes a surprising amount of the design.

2Frames and GOPs

Not all frames are equal. H.264 has three kinds, distinguished by what they are allowed to predict from:

  • I frames predict only from themselves (spatial prediction). Expensive, maybe 5 to 10x the bits of a P frame, but they are random-access points where a decoder can start cold. Seeking and channel switching jump to I frames, and recovery from packet loss restarts on one.
  • P frames predict from earlier frames. The workhorses.
  • B frames predict from earlier and later frames, averaging both directions. Cheapest of all, often 4 to 10x smaller than P frames. The catch is that a B frame needs its future reference decoded first, so frames are transmitted out of display order and the decoder reorders them, which adds latency.

The distance between I frames, the keyframe interval, is a straight trade: long GOPs compress better (fewer expensive I frames) but make seeking coarser and recovery from packet loss slower. Streaming services also cut their segment boundaries at I frames, which is why adaptive players can only switch quality every couple of seconds.

A special I frame called an IDR (instantaneous decoder refresh) additionally forbids any later frame from referencing anything before it. That fully resets decoder state, making the point truly seekable. One of the oldest encoder-side smarts is placing IDRs at scene cuts, where a full refresh was going to be nearly free anyway because nothing predicts well across a cut. Hold that thought for the last section.

3Intra prediction

Even a frame with no past can avoid coding raw pixels. Intra prediction builds each block from its already-decoded neighbors, the row of pixels above and the column to the left, by projecting them across the block in one of several directions.

Luma blocks come in two intra flavors: 16x16 with four simple modes (vertical, horizontal, DC, plane) for flat regions, and 4x4 with nine modes for detailed ones. High profile adds 8x8. The prediction is rarely exact, and it doesn't have to be. If a gradient sky is predicted within a few code values, the leftover error is tiny and nearly free to code.

4Motion compensation

Inter prediction is where the big money is. For each block, the encoder searches the reference frame for the patch that matches best, then transmits just a motion vector, the (x, y) offset to that patch, and the residual error. A panning shot of a stadium crowd, tens of thousands of "detailed" pixels, can cost almost nothing: every block is "same as last frame, shifted 4 pixels left."

A few details make this far better than the naive version:

  • Variable block sizes. A 16x16 macroblock can split into 16x8, 8x16, 8x8, and each 8x8 further down to 4x4. Every partition gets its own vector. Smooth backgrounds use one big cheap vector; the boundary of a moving arm gets a fistful of small precise ones.
  • Quarter-pixel precision. Vectors resolve to 0.25 px. Half-pel positions come from a 6-tap filter, quarter-pel from averaging. This alone is worth a large slice of H.264's advantage over older codecs.
  • Multiple reference frames. Up to 16. Useful when content occludes and reappears: the wall behind a passing truck is still sitting, perfectly intact, in a frame from half a second ago.
  • Predicted vectors. Neighboring blocks usually move together, so each vector is coded as a difference from the median of its neighbors' vectors. Uniform motion costs almost zero bits.
  • Skip mode. The best block is the one you never send. If the predicted vector with no residual is good enough, a macroblock costs roughly one bit. In quiet scenes most of the frame is skips.

Motion search is also where encoder CPU time goes. The spec doesn't care how you find the vector, only how you encode it, so search strategy (diamond, hexagon, exhaustive) is a pure speed-versus-quality knob and one of the main things separating fast presets from slow ones.

5The integer transform

Part one covers what a transform does and lets you quantize a real frame at any QP. Two things about H.264's version are worth knowing beyond that.

The transform is exact. Earlier standards specified a real discrete cosine transform, which every decoder implemented in floating point and no two implemented identically. Tiny differences accumulated through the prediction loop, so a long sequence could visibly drift away from what the encoder intended. H.264 specifies an integer approximation instead: small whole numbers, exact in 16-bit arithmetic, bit-identical on every conformant decoder. It is slightly worse than a true DCT at compacting energy and enormously better at being reproducible, and that trade is what makes the recon-match gate this project relies on possible at all — the encoder can assert its own reconstruction equals a decoder's, bit for bit.

Two block sizes, chosen per macroblock. Baseline H.264 has only the 4x4 transform. High profile adds 8x8, and the encoder picks between them for every macroblock. 4x4 follows edges without smearing them; 8x8 packs smooth or finely textured regions into fewer coefficients. Neither wins everywhere, so the choice is a rate-distortion decision like any other (the size of the choice), and getting it right is worth real bitrate.

QP itself runs 0 to 51 and doubles the step every 6 points, so QP 28 codes at four times the step of QP 16. Everything called rate control is ultimately a policy for choosing it.

6Entropy coding

Everything so far produced symbols: modes, motion vector differences, quantized coefficients. Entropy coding turns them into the actual bitstream, losslessly, by giving short codes to likely symbols. H.264 ships two engines, and the choice is a profile flag:

  • CAVLC (context-adaptive variable-length coding) assigns each symbol a whole number of bits from lookup tables, adapting the table choice to context, like how many coefficients the neighboring blocks had. Simple, fast, and every symbol costs at least one bit.
  • CABAC (context-adaptive binary arithmetic coding) is the interesting one. Every syntax element is decomposed into binary decisions, and each decision gets a probability model that updates as the stream is coded, over 400 separate contexts. An arithmetic coder then packs the decisions at their true information content, so a bin that is 95% predictable costs about 0.07 bits. Highly skewed symbols, which is most of them, get dramatically cheaper.

CABAC buys roughly 10 to 15% bitrate at the same quality, one of the largest single wins in the standard, paid for with a serial, branch-heavy decode loop that resists parallelism. That tension, compression versus decodability at speed, shaped every codec since; H.265 restructured its CABAC specifically so hardware could keep up at 4K.

7The deblocking filter

Block-based coding has a signature failure: every block quantized independently means visible seams at block edges, the classic "blocky video" look. H.264 builds the fix into the standard. An adaptive smoothing filter runs along every 4x4 block edge, with strength chosen per edge from coding conditions (intra blocks get stronger filtering, high QP gets stronger filtering) and clever thresholds that distinguish "quantization seam" from "real edge of an object," so it smooths the first and preserves the second.

The word that matters is in-loop. The filtered frame is what goes into the reference buffer, so predictions are made from cleaned-up pixels and the improvement compounds frame over frame. It's worth several percent bitrate and a large subjective improvement at low rates, and it's why H.264 at starvation bitrates goes soft rather than shattering into tiles the way MPEG-2 did.

8The size of the choice

Part one covers rate-distortion optimization: every candidate encoding costs J = D + λ·R, minimise J, and λ comes from QP. What is worth adding here is how many candidates H.264 actually creates, because that is the reason two conformant encoders differ so much.

For one macroblock: intra or inter; nine directional intra modes at 4x4 and 8x8 and four at 16x16; seven partition shapes down to 4x4; up to sixteen reference frames, each with its own motion vector at quarter-pixel accuracy; one or two prediction directions; skip or code; 4x4 or 8x8 transform; and which coefficients survive quantization. The combinations run to the hundreds before the encoder has looked at a second macroblock, and there are 8160 macroblocks in a 1080p frame.

No encoder searches that space. Every one of them is a set of decisions about which parts not to look at, which is what a preset is: fast presets price candidates with cheap proxies such as the sum of absolute transformed differences and prune hard, slow presets actually encode candidates to measure what they truly cost. Same specification, wildly different shopping strategies. This is the entire space this project works in.

9What the standard does not say

Rate control, mode decision, motion search, lookahead, adaptive quantization, psychovisual tuning: none of it is in H.264. The specification defines what a conformant bitstream decodes to and says nothing about how an encoder chose it. Part one covers the five rate-control modes and how quality gets measured, and neither topic is H.264-specific.

That silence is the whole opportunity. Every decoder already deployed keeps working no matter how much better the encoder gets, which means twenty-three years after the format froze there is still room to move — and it is where all of this project's work happens. The measured results are what came of it.

10The future, and yah264

H.264 is twenty-three years old and is still the most compatible video format on Earth. Its successors H.265, AV1, and VVC buy better compression with far more expensive tools, but the underappreciated fact is that the standard only fixes the decoder. Inside the fixed syntax, the encoder's decisions, where to spend, what to predict, what to throw away, are wide open. Most of what changed video quality in the last decade was encoder-side intelligence, not new syntax: CRF, mbtree, psy-RD, per-title encoding. That is the thesis of yah264, our from-scratch H.264 encoder. The decoder spec is settled, and the deciding is not. It already does the full pipeline above on Apple Silicon: I/P/B frames, quarter-pel motion, RD mode decision, deblocking, and both entropy coders, CAVLC and CABAC. What follows is where we think the remaining headroom is.

Shot-based encoding, in one pass

The next planned feature

A movie isn't one signal, it's a few hundred shots with wildly different costs: a static close-up might look perfect at 1 Mb/s while a rain-soaked chase needs 8. Netflix proved the value of exploiting this: encode every shot at many resolution and QP combinations, keep each shot's best rate-quality tradeoffs (the convex hull), then pick one operating point per shot so the whole title is optimal. Reported gains: 17% over fixed-QP, over 50% versus ordinary two-pass VBR. The catch is dozens of trial encodes per shot, run by a cluster-scale orchestrator.

The research since suggests the trial encodes are mostly avoidable: recent work predicts a shot's convex hull from its content with a small model, losing only about 0.26% BD-rate while skipping 62% of the encodes. yah264's plan collapses the whole thing into the encoder: detect shots in the lookahead, score their complexity from motion-search statistics the encoder already computes, and pick per-shot QP (later, per-shot resolution for streaming ladders) in a single pass. The full plan lives in docs/shot-based-plan.md.

Film grain synthesis

Large savings on film content, playable today

Film grain is the most expensive thing a codec can face: high-energy noise, different every frame, that motion compensation can't predict and quantization visibly destroys. AV1 made the fix famous: denoise the source, encode the clean video cheaply, transmit a statistical description of the grain, and have the decoder resynthesize it. Almost nobody knows H.264 has supported this since 2004, via the film grain characteristics SEI message, and FFmpeg-based players have rendered it since 2021. No mainstream H.264 encoder ships it. yah264 writing that SEI would make it the first, with graceful degradation (clean video, no grain) everywhere else.

Perception-driven bit placement

Reported 7 to 22% bitrate savings in recent research

Adaptive quantization spends bits where errors are visible. The next step is spending them where people actually look. Saliency models, even cheap heuristic ones built from motion contrast, can steer per-block QP so the face in focus gets the bits the out-of-focus background was hoarding. Recent work on newer codecs (PAVEN, SJ-PVC) reports 7 to 22% bitrate savings at equal subjective quality from saliency combined with just-noticeable-difference models. The mechanism, per-macroblock QP offsets, drops straight into H.264.

Learned encoder decisions

Speed and consistency, no bitstream changes

A small trained model beats a hand-tuned heuristic in three places the decoder never sees. Predicting coded frame size from lookahead features tightens rate control and calms QP oscillation, and Google has shipped versions of this in production encoders. Pruning the mode search to the candidates likely to win is reported to speed newer codecs up 30 to 50% at about 1% quality cost. And a prefilter trained end-to-end through a differentiable codec proxy, following Google's "sandwiched compression" work, makes frames cheaper to encode before the codec ever sees them. On Apple Silicon these run on the neural engine while the CPU encodes.

Cleaner references, borrowed from AV1

Standard-compliant, encoder-side only

SVT-AV1 motion-compensation-filters a frame before encoding it as a reference, so predictions come from a temporally denoised image and residuals shrink everywhere downstream. Nothing about that trick is AV1-specific; it never touches the bitstream. Ported to H.264 it pairs beautifully with grain synthesis: temporal filtering removes exactly the noise the grain SEI puts back.

The through-line is that the codec war's front moved. It used to be syntax, fought in standards committees over a decade per round. Now the advantage is in the decisions, measured by perceptual metrics, informed by lookahead and learned models, and shippable in software next month with full compatibility back to a 2004 decoder. A twenty-three-year-old bitstream format with a modern brain is a genuinely competitive thing to build. That's the bet.
